Kroger's $400M E-commerce Pivot: Closing Automated Centers for Store-Based Fulfillment

In a bold strategic shift, Kroger announced it will close three of its high-tech automated fulfillment centers in January 2025, taking a massive $2.6 billion impairment charge while pivoting toward a hybrid model of store-based fulfillment and third-party delivery partnerships. The move signals a fundamental rethinking of grocery e-commerce economics-and raises critical questions about the viability of dedicated automation infrastructure in an industry with razor-thin margins.

The $400 Million Profitability Play
Kroger's e-commerce dilemma is one familiar to many traditional retailers: strong growth but stubborn losses. Despite reporting 16% e-commerce sales growth in Q2 2025 and five consecutive quarters of double-digit expansion, the division remains unprofitable. Interim CEO Ron Sargent is taking decisive action to change that trajectory, targeting approximately $400 million in profitability improvements by 2026.

The centerpiece of this strategy is abandoning the capital-intensive automated fulfillment center model-at least in lower-density markets. After a "comprehensive review" of its Ocado-powered CFC network, Kroger concluded that three facilities simply weren't generating enough volume to justify their operational costs.

The Ocado Partnership Evolves
Kroger's partnership with UK automation specialist Ocado launched in 2018 with high expectations. The companies envisioned a network of cutting-edge automated fulfillment centers that would revolutionize online grocery delivery. But after opening several CFCs, Kroger paused new rollouts in 2023.

While two additional centers in Charlotte and Phoenix are still planned for 2026, the closure of three existing facilities represents a significant scaling back of the original vision. The message is clear: automation works where density justifies the investment, but it's not a universal solution.

Three Centers, One Decision
The three facilities slated for closure represent different stages of Kroger's automation journey, but all share a common challenge: insufficient order density to achieve profitability.

Location	Opened	Status
Pleasant Prairie, Wisconsin	June 2022	Closing January 2025
Frederick, Maryland	June 2023	Closing January 2025
Groveland, Florida	June 2021	Closing January 2025
The Store-Based Alternative
Rather than centralized mega-fulfillment centers, Kroger is betting on "capital-light, store-based automation in high-volume markets." This approach leverages existing infrastructure-stores are already paying rent, employing staff, and receiving inventory. Adding fulfillment capabilities becomes an incremental cost rather than a massive capital investment.

The economics are compelling: store-based fulfillment can serve local demand with faster delivery times while avoiding the fixed costs of dedicated facilities. For customers in markets losing CFC coverage, this means shifting to pickup or delivery fulfilled from nearby stores-potentially with even shorter lead times.

Capital-Light Innovation
Kroger's pilot of "capital-light, store-based automation" represents a middle path between manual picking and full warehouse automation. Think micro-fulfillment systems integrated into existing stores rather than purpose-built robotic warehouses. It's automation that scales with demand rather than requiring massive upfront bets.

Embracing the Platform Economy
Perhaps the most telling aspect of Kroger's pivot is its deepening embrace of third-party delivery platforms. Rather than owning the entire customer experience end-to-end, Kroger is partnering with established players who have already solved the last-mile delivery challenge.

Partner	Role	Key Feature
Instacart	Primary delivery fulfillment provider	AI-powered shopping assistant
DoorDash	Delivery from ~2,700 stores	Expanded coverage
Uber	Growing partnership	Launch early 2026
Instacart's new AI shopping assistant-piloted within Kroger's mobile app-is particularly noteworthy. It represents a bet that conversational commerce could be the key to increasing basket sizes and frequency without Kroger having to build the AI infrastructure itself. Meanwhile, partnerships with DoorDash and Uber expand last-mile delivery coverage without capital investment.

What This Means for Grocery E-commerce
1. Automation Isn't One-Size-Fits-All
The closure of these centers doesn't mean automation failed-it means that massive centralized automation requires density that many markets simply don't have. Store-based micro-fulfillment may be a more scalable solution for most retailers.
2. Own What Differentiates, Partner for the Rest
Kroger's pivot suggests that owning product, stores, and customer relationships is sufficient-last-mile delivery and AI shopping assistants can be outsourced to specialists. This is the classic "build vs. buy" decision, and Kroger is choosing to buy.
3. Profitability Over Growth
Taking a $2.6B charge to improve profitability by $400M annually demonstrates a clear prioritization: sustainable economics matter more than aggressive expansion. This may signal a broader shift in retail e-commerce from growth-at-any-cost to disciplined profitability.
4. AI as the New Competitive Frontier
While pulling back on physical automation, Kroger is leaning into AI-powered shopping experiences via Instacart. The implication: software-driven personalization and convenience may deliver better ROI than robotic warehouses in many contexts.
The Human Element
Lost in the strategic discussion are the workers at these three closing centers. While Kroger hasn't detailed employment impacts, the closure of three facilities will inevitably affect jobs. The company's shift to store-based fulfillment may absorb some workers, but the transition won't be seamless.

It's a reminder that automation decisions have real human consequences-and that the "future of work" in retail remains uncertain as companies experiment with different fulfillment models.

Looking Ahead: A New Playbook Emerges
Kroger's strategic reset offers a roadmap for other grocers navigating the e-commerce profitability challenge:

•
Start with store-based fulfillment as the default, adding dedicated automation only in proven high-density markets
•
Partner with delivery platforms rather than building proprietary last-mile networks
•
Leverage AI to increase basket sizes and frequency through better personalization and convenience
•
Be willing to take write-downs on infrastructure that isn't working rather than throwing good money after bad
The grocery industry is watching closely. If Kroger achieves its $400M profitability target by 2026, expect other retailers to follow this hybrid model. If the pivot stumbles, it may validate the dedicated-facility approach in more markets than initially thought.

The Bottom Line
Kroger's $400M profitability pivot isn't just about closing three warehouses-it's about fundamentally rethinking what infrastructure makes sense for profitable grocery e-commerce. By embracing store-based fulfillment, third-party delivery, and AI-powered shopping experiences, Kroger is betting that a lighter, more flexible model will outperform capital-intensive automation in most markets.

The $2.6B write-down is painful, but it may be the price of avoiding an even costlier strategic dead end. Sometimes the best path forward requires acknowledging what isn't working and having the courage to change course. For Kroger-and the broader grocery industry-the next two years will reveal whether this pivot was brilliant or premature.
