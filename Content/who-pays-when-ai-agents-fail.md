Who's Liable When AI Agents Make Mistakes?
As agentic commerce grows, so do questions about who's responsible when AI makes mistakes. The real issue isn't legal risk - it's that most teams haven't talked about accountability at all.

By Robert Hu
•
Jan 6, 2026

Start with the Conversation
In the past few months, I've had a version of the same conversation with at least a dozen founders and operators. It usually starts with excitement about agentic commerce - AI systems that can browse, compare, and even buy on behalf of customers. But then, pretty quickly, the tone shifts.

"What happens when the agent messes up?"

"Who's on the hook if it recommends the wrong thing?"

"Are we liable if the AI misreads a customer's intent?"

These aren't hypothetical edge cases anymore. As AI agents start making real decisions in commerce - recommending products, initiating purchases, handling returns - the question of accountability is becoming urgent.

The Liability Conversation Everyone's Avoiding
Here's what I keep noticing: teams are deploying AI automation before they've had a single internal conversation about responsibility. Not because they don't care, but because the pressure to experiment is intense. There's a fear of being left behind, of not having an "AI strategy" to point to.

So they ship something. A chatbot that can place orders. A recommendation engine with more autonomy. An agent that handles customer service escalations.

And then, when something goes wrong - a refund processed incorrectly, a product recommended that shouldn't have been, a customer upset by an interaction that felt "off" - the finger-pointing begins.

The problem isn't that teams lack legal frameworks for AI liability. It's that they haven't agreed on what "accountability" even means internally before deploying automation.
Ambiguity Amplifies Risk Perception
When there's no clear owner for AI decisions, every mistake feels bigger than it is. That's because ambiguity creates anxiety - not just for customers, but for the teams managing these systems.

Consider a scenario: an AI shopping agent recommends a product that a customer later returns. Was that a bad recommendation? A mismatch in preference data? A failure in the underlying model? Or just normal customer behavior that would have happened anyway?

Without clarity on what the AI is supposed to optimize for - and who's responsible for monitoring its decisions - every outcome becomes a source of debate. And that debate erodes trust, both internally and externally.

The Pattern I Keep Seeing
Here's how it usually unfolds:

Excitement phase: Team deploys AI automation with minimal guardrails, focused on proving value fast.
First incident: Something goes wrong - a customer complains, a transaction fails, or an edge case surfaces.
Blame scramble: No one knows who should own the fix. Engineering says it's a product issue. Product says it's an operations issue. Operations says they weren't told the AI could do that.
Overcorrection: Leadership pulls back on AI autonomy entirely, afraid of more incidents. Progress stalls.
The irony is that the incident itself was often minor. What made it painful was the lack of clarity around responsibility.

Where Trust and Clarity Intersect
Customer trust in AI-driven experiences isn't just about whether the AI gets things right. It's about whether customers feel like someone is accountable when it doesn't.

Think about how traditional commerce handles mistakes. If a store associate recommends the wrong product, there's a clear path to resolution - a manager to talk to, a return process that feels human. The mistake is annoying, but it doesn't break trust because there's a visible system for accountability.

With AI agents, that visibility disappears. When an algorithm makes a decision, customers don't know who to hold responsible. And if the brand can't answer that question clearly either, trust erodes fast.

The Trust Equation
Customer trust in AI commerce = (value delivered) + (clarity when things go wrong). You can't control every outcome, but you can control how clearly you own them.

This Isn't Just a Payments Problem
A lot of the conversation around AI liability has focused on payments - who's responsible when an agent makes a fraudulent purchase, or when a transaction fails. And those are real concerns. But they're also the most visible, most regulated part of the stack.

The harder problems are upstream. They're about:

Recommendation accountability: When an AI suggests products that don't fit, who owns that mismatch?
Intent interpretation: When an agent misreads what a customer wants, how do you course-correct?
Escalation paths: When the AI hits its limits, how seamlessly does it hand off to a human?
Feedback loops: How do you know if the AI's decisions are actually improving over time?
These aren't legal questions. They're strategic clarity questions. And most teams haven't answered them.

A Question to Structure Your Planning
If you're deploying AI agents in any part of your commerce stack, here's a question worth sitting with:

"If this AI makes a decision that costs us a customer, who in the room right now would own the resolution - and do they know that?"
It's not a trick question. And there's no single right answer. But the act of asking it - out loud, with the relevant stakeholders present - tends to surface gaps that no one realized existed.

Maybe it reveals that product and support haven't aligned on escalation protocols. Maybe it shows that engineering built monitoring, but no one's actually watching the dashboard. Maybe it just highlights that "ownership" has been assumed but never documented.

Whatever it surfaces, you're better off knowing now.

The Real Risk Isn't AI Mistakes
Here's the thing: AI agents will make mistakes. That's not a bug in the system - it's the nature of probabilistic systems operating in complex environments. The question isn't how to eliminate errors. It's how to build systems - organizational, not just technical - that can absorb them without breaking trust.

The brands that win in agentic commerce won't be the ones with perfect AI. They'll be the ones who've done the unglamorous work of defining accountability before they need it. Who have clear escalation paths that customers actually experience as responsive. Who treat AI decisions as shared team outputs, not black-box magic.

That's not a prediction about the future. It's an observation about what's already separating the teams I talk to who feel confident from the ones who feel stuck.

Start with the Conversation
If you take one thing from this post, let it be this: the liability conversation isn't a legal exercise to hand off to counsel. It's a strategic clarity exercise that belongs in your next team meeting.

Before you ship the next AI feature, before you expand your agent's autonomy, before you add another decision to the automation layer - ask the accountability question. Out loud. With everyone in the room.

You might be surprised what surfaces.
